# Understanding Univariate Regression

## Introduction

In this blog I want to write about **Univariate Regression**. At core this is a very simple and foundational idea for many machine learning and statistical tasks. I want to work through the derivation, include some visualisation, and focus on what the result is really saying.

## Setup

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two real random variables (not necessarily independent) with two moments and such that

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) \neq 0.
$$

We observe paired samples $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$ and want to approximate $Y$ using a linear function of $X$:

$$
\hat{y} = a + b x.
$$

## What does “best fit” mean?

For each data point $x_i$, our model predicts

$$
\hat{y}_i = a + b x_i,
$$

and the residual (vertical error) is

$$
e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i.
$$

### Why this distance?

There are many possible distances (vertical, orthogonal, absolute, squared, etc.).

In ordinary least squares, we use **squared vertical residuals** and minimise their sum:

$$
S(a,b)
= \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - (a + b x_i)\big)^2.
$$

This choice is popular because it’s smooth, gives a clean closed-form solution, and later connects to maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.

---

## Derivation (population view)

Up to now, we haven’t assumed any *noise distribution* (Gaussian, etc.).  
Instead, we take a **random variable viewpoint**:

- $X$ and $Y$ are real random variables with finite second moments,
- we search over all linear predictors $g(x)=a+bx$,
- and we choose the one that minimises the **mean squared prediction error**.

### The objective: best linear predictor in MSE

Define

$$
J(a,b)
:= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(Y-(a+bX))^2\right].
$$

Minimising $J$ is a purely mechanical optimisation problem: we are choosing the “closest” linear function to $Y$ in the $L^2$ (mean-square) sense.

---

### Step 1: optimise over $a$

Differentiate and set to zero:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial a}
= -2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y-(a+bX)\right]=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb{E}[Y]=a+b\,\mathbb{E}[X].
$$

So the optimal intercept must satisfy

$$
a^\star = \mathbb{E}[Y]-b\,\mathbb{E}[X].
$$

**Interpretation:** the fitted line passes through the “mean point”
$(\mathbb{E}[X],\mathbb{E}[Y])$.

---

### Step 2: optimise over $b$

Differentiate w.r.t. $b$ and set to zero:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}
= -2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[X\,(Y-(a+bX))\right]=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb{E}[XY]=a\,\mathbb{E}[X]+b\,\mathbb{E}[X^2].
$$

Substitute $a^\star=\mathbb{E}[Y]-b\,\mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[XY]
= (\mathbb{E}[Y]-b\,\mathbb{E}[X])\mathbb{E}[X]+b\,\mathbb{E}[X^2].
$$

Rearrange:

$$
\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]
= b\left(\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2\right).
$$

Recognise covariance and variance:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = b\,\operatorname{Var}(X).
$$

So the optimal slope is

$$
b^\star = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)},
\qquad
a^\star = \mathbb{E}[Y]-b^\star\mathbb{E}[X].
$$

### Why we need $\operatorname{Var}(X)\neq 0$

If $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0$, then $X$ is constant almost surely.  
There is no “spread” in $X$, so a slope is not identifiable: many lines give the same predictions on the support of $X$.

That’s why the ratio $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X)$ needs a nonzero denominator.

---

## From random variables to data (Monte Carlo + Law of Large Numbers)

In practice, we do not know $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$, or $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.  
We only observe samples $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$, which you can think of as i.i.d. draws from the joint distribution of $(X,Y)$.

The key idea is that expectations can be approximated by sample averages:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)]
\approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i,y_i).
$$

This is exactly the **Monte Carlo principle**, and it is justified by the **Law of Large Numbers**: as $n\to\infty$, the sample average converges to the true expectation (under mild conditions).

So the population objective

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(Y-(a+bX))^2\right]
$$

is approximated by the empirical objective

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i-(a+bx_i)\right)^2,
$$

which is the usual least-squares loss used in machine learning.
